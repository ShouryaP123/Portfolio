PROJECT 01 OF 03VTOL_RECON_V1

VTOL_RECON_V1

A scratch-built VTOL aircraft. Personal project, sole engineer.

I wanted an aircraft that could take off vertically like a quadcopter but still cruise efficiently like a plane, so I designed and built a quadplane: four lift rotors on carbon booms for hover, and a pusher motor with a 1550 mm wing for forward flight. I did all of it myself, working in order from the aero and stability sizing in XFLR5 and the propulsion sizing in eCalc, through a Python model for the spar, and then the CAD, the 3D printing, and the wiring.

I sized the airframe to an 1781 g target and it came out at 1849 g on the scale once it was actually built, about 3.8% heavier. The extra weight came from print tolerances, adhesive, and hardware I hadn't accounted for in the model. That gap is small enough that I trust my component mass build up, and it's the first number I check on the next build.

Aerodynamics and Stability

XFLR5, vortex lattice and lifting line

This was the first thing I did, before any CAD or printing, because the geometry decisions here are the ones that are expensive to undo later. I built the aircraft in XFLR5 to check that it would actually be stable. The thing I cared about most was the tail. Too small and the plane is twitchy or divergent in pitch, too big and I'm carrying drag and weight I don't need. I ended up with a 698 mm tail position and a 680.5 mm elevator lever arm, which gives a tail volume coefficient of 0.52 against the 0.29 m² wing.

I then ran a fixed lift sweep from −2° to 14°. The pitching moment slope comes out negative, which is what I was looking for. If the nose pitches up, the aircraft generates a moment that pushes it back down instead of running away. Lift to drag peaks around 4 to 5 degrees, so that is the angle I aimed to trim for in cruise.

The lift curve is straight across the whole sweep, but that is a property of the method rather than a finding. A vortex lattice solution is inviscid, so it keeps producing linear lift well past the angle where a real wing would stall. What I took from this analysis was the lift curve slope, the trim point, and the moment behavior, not a stall angle. I also ran it at the 1781 g design weight, so the as-built aircraft is slightly heavier than what's plotted here, which puts the real trim speed a little above what the model says.

Propulsion Sizing

eCalc, hover case

A quadplane has to be sized twice, because hover and cruise ask for completely different things. In hover the four rotors carry the entire weight of the aircraft, so I needed thrust well above 1:1 to have any control authority left over. In cruise the wing carries the weight and the pusher only has to overcome drag, so thrust below 1:1 is fine and expected. Sizing both to the same target would have meant carrying a much heavier propulsion system than the aircraft needs.

Hover: four EMAX 2807 motors on 8×4 props

The four lift motors do nothing once the aircraft is in cruise, so every gram of that system is dead weight in forward flight. That tension, enough hover authority without over building the lift system, drove most of the motor and prop selection.

Known limitation

The red line at the top of that output is eCalc predicting a 92°C motor case temperature at maximum, past where I'd want a motor sitting for a long time. For the short reconnaissance flights I designed this around, running the pusher near full power only in climb, I accepted it. It's the clearest limitation in the current setup, and if I fly longer missions the fix is either a lower Kv motor on a bigger prop, or actual airflow over the motor can.

Building It

CAD, FDM printing, and avionics integration

With the aero and propulsion settled, I moved into CAD and started printing. The airframe is printed in sections and joined over carbon tubes. The wing splits into halves that slide onto a spar running through both, and the booms carrying the lift motors are separate tubes clamped to printed mounts. Printing in sections meant I could fit parts on the bed and reprint one piece after a crash instead of the whole wing, which mattered more than I expected once I started flying it.

I did the wiring myself, running power from the pack through the ESCs out to five motors, and I checked continuity and voltage with a multimeter before ever putting a prop on. The extra 68 g over my design estimate came out of exactly this stage: adhesive at the joints, connectors, and printed parts that came off the bed a little heavier than the model said.

PROJECT 02 OF 03FRONT WING AIRFOIL

Front Wing Airfoil Optimization

Formula Buckeyes. 2D CFD in STAR-CCM+.

I started from the NASA Apex 16 (CR-20106), an airfoil built for high lift in ground effect, and ran it in STAR-CCM+ to see how much more I could get out of it. The goal was the highest lift to drag ratio I could reach at a fixed operating condition, with a ground plane in the domain so that ground effect was part of the problem rather than a free air result.

The baseline gave a lift to drag around 81 at zero incidence. Sweeping angle of attack, the section did best at −4°, which brought it to roughly 108. From there I thinned the section: 10% thinner got me to about 115, and 15% thinner, the configuration I settled on, came out at 115.7, which the solver reports from an average lift coefficient of 1.2544 against an average drag coefficient of 0.010841. Thinning cuts the pressure drag from the thick forward shape faster than it gives up lift at this operating point, which is why the ratio keeps climbing.

What the Residuals Show

Solver convergence and the limits of this study

I want to be straight about how far I'd push these numbers. The residuals drop several orders of magnitude and then settle into a steady oscillation instead of flattening out. Running a steady solver on a flow that is not actually steady gives exactly this signature: the solution reaches a repeating unsteadiness rather than a converged state. Given the low velocity region sitting near the leading edge on the pressure side, some unsteady separation there is the likely source.

Because of that, and because I didn't run a formal mesh independence study, I treat 115.7 as a solid comparison between configurations that were all run identically, rather than an absolute number I would hand to someone building the part. The trend across the sweep is what I trust: thinner sections did better, and −4° beat every other angle I tried.

This is also a two dimensional sectional analysis, so it says nothing about tip vortices or the three dimensional flow a real finite wing would see. Extending it to 3D and running a mesh study are the two things I would do next.

PROJECT 03 OF 03FRONT WING MOUNT

Front Wing Mount Load Path Study

Formula Buckeyes. CAD and topology optimization in Ansys 2024 R2.

The front wing on our Formula car bolts to the chassis through a swan neck bracket, and that bracket is a common failure point. The aero load does not act at the bolts. It acts out at the wing, offset from the plane the bolts sit in, so the bracket has to carry both the force itself and the moment that offset creates. The direct force is reacted in shear and bearing at the holes, while the moment shows up as a tension and compression couple across the bolt pattern: under downforce the bolts on one side of the pattern get pulled while the opposite side is pushed into the chassis.

To get the bolt positions right I worked from a single known anchor point on the geometry and used relative distances to place every other hole in the car's coordinate frame, rather than measuring each one independently. Moment arms are the lever lengths in that calculation, so an error in the reference point would propagate into every moment I computed.

First design

I ran a topology optimization on this design to see how much of it was actually carrying load. The result came back with material dropping out over a large share of the plate, which told me the bracket was heavier than the load case required. That costs twice on a Formula car: it is mass hung off the front of the chassis doing nothing for stiffness, and it is material and machining time we pay for on a part that did not need to be that size.

Second Design and Reoptimization

Topology optimization on the revised bracket

Knowing where the load actually went, I drew a second bracket, cutting the profile back in the regions the first optimization had emptied out while keeping section depth around the bolt clusters. Then I reran the optimization on that new geometry to check the revision against the same load case. The figure below is that second run, which is why its outline does not match the CAD on the previous design.

What the solver keeps is a truss, and that is the shape the load case predicts. A moment across the bolt pattern is reacted by a tension and compression pair separated by as much depth as the part allows, so material belongs at the extreme upper and lower edges, with bracing to tie them together and feed load into the chassis. The middle of a section in bending sits near zero stress, which is why it hollows out. Getting a result that agrees with the hand analysis is the check that the model and load case were set up correctly.

A topology optimization returns a density field, not a part. I used these two runs to find the load path and to size the revision, and I stopped there. Turning the result into something that can be made means smoothing the field into clean CAD and running a static stress check on that final geometry against the real load case, which is the next step if the design gets carried forward.
